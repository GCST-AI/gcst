01 · Definition

What the SDLC actually is

The software development life cycle is the ordered list of things that have to happen for an idea to become software real people use, and keep using. Not a philosophy. Not a certification. A list, with an order, that repeats. You can learn the whole shape of it tonight, and you will use it for the rest of your working life.

“The software development lifecycle (SDLC) is a structured and iterative methodology used by development teams to build, deliver and maintain high-quality and cost-effective software systems.” IBM · What is the software development life cycle?

Read that again and notice two words, iterative and maintain. Both are doing real work. The cycle does not stop at “we finished it”, and it does not run once. That is good news for you. It means a weak first attempt is normal and recoverable, not a verdict on you.

IBM breaks it down as “distinct, repeatable, interdependent phases”, where each phase has its own objectives and deliverables that guide the next phase. That last part is the whole mechanism. A phase is not finished because time ran out. It is finished because it produced the thing the next phase needs.

This hands you a test you can apply without any experience. When somebody asks “are we done with design?”, the answer is not how anyone feels about it. It is can somebody start coding from what design produced? If no, design is not done. Learn that one move and you are already ahead of most first projects.

Why it exists

Software is built by several people, over months, for users who are not in the room. The SDLC is what lets those three facts coexist without the project quietly falling apart. It was built by people who watched projects fail and wrote down why.

What it gives you

IBM lists better software, higher productivity, less risk, more transparency, better collaboration, saner use of time and happier users. Microsoft's list is the same list with different nouns. Two rivals agreeing this closely is worth noticing.

What it is not

A guarantee. You can run every phase properly and still build something nobody wants. The cycle protects you from chaos, not from being wrong. Talking to users is what protects you from being wrong.

Stop and think · 01

You are one person, building one page, over one weekend. Is the SDLC overhead you should skip?

Two minutes. Write one sentence before you open this.

What I was listening for

You cannot skip it. You can only skip writing it down.

Even alone on a Saturday you decide what to build, work out what it needs, sketch it, code it, check it, put it somewhere, and fix it when it breaks. That is seven phases. They happen in your head in four seconds each, and you have been doing it correctly for years without a name for it.

The document is the optional part. The phases are not. What changes with team size and project length is how much has to leave your head and become text another person can read. You are on a pod, and your project runs to November, so a fair amount of it does.

02 · Reading the diagrams

Six phases or seven? Why the diagrams disagree

Search for “SDLC” and you get a hundred coloured wheels that do not match. Six stages here, seven there, one calls it coding and the next calls it implementation. Students lose an hour deciding which one is correct. Save yourself the hour. None of them is correct, because it is not that kind of question.

Both pictures describe the same work. They differ on where they draw the lines, and the lines are a naming convention, not a law. Take a habit from this that will serve you in every subject you study. When two sources disagree on the count, compare the deliverables, not the labels. If both produce a requirements document, both have an analysis phase, whatever it is called.

Where it does matter, the two most careful sources agree. IBM and Microsoft both land on seven, and on the same seven.

#IBMMicrosoftThe deliverable both name
1PlanningPlanningScope, goals, a project plan
2AnalysisAnalysisDetailed requirements
3DesignDesignArchitecture, mockups, an SDD
4CodingDevelopmentWorking software
5TestingTestingTest results, bug reports
6DeploymentDeploymentSoftware users can reach
7MaintenanceMaintenancePatches and improvements

One word differs out of seven. That is the entire disagreement between two of the largest software companies on earth, and it is a synonym. So stop worrying about which diagram to trust. Use these seven for the rest of the page and for your project.

03 · Phase 01
01

Planning

Planning decides what the project is for, how big it is allowed to be, and what it is not going to do. IBM is blunt about that second half. The phase aims “to both pinpoint the project's goals and establish what the project does not need, which helps prevent it from becoming too bloated.”

The output is usually an initial software requirement specification, an SRS, describing what the software does, what it needs, what could go wrong and roughly when it happens. Microsoft frames the same phase around feasibility. Is this doable, by these people, in this time. Answering that honestly on day one is a strength, not a lack of ambition.

Deliverable A written scope: what it does, who for, and an explicit list of what you are not building.
In your pod, this week

Write the one sentence that goes in <p class="summary"> on your index.html. Then list, in your own notes, five things people will ask for that you are refusing to build. Twenty minutes of work that pays you back all semester.

The test of a good scope

Somebody outside your pod reads the sentence and can tell you, correctly, what your project does. If they guess wrong, the sentence is the problem, not them. Rewrite it and try again on someone new.

How it fails

No written non-goals. Every suggestion in the group chat becomes a feature, because nothing on paper says it should not. That is scope creep, and it ends more student projects than difficulty ever does.

Stop and think · 02

Name three things your project will deliberately not do. If you cannot, what does that tell you about your scope?

Three minutes. Write them down properly, they go in your notes.

What I was listening for

It tells you your scope is everything, which is the same as no scope at all.

A boundary you cannot describe is a boundary you cannot defend. In week seven somebody will say “it should also do X, that is easy”, and with nothing written down, the honest answer is yes.

Good non-goals are specific and slightly painful. “No login.” “English only for now.” “Works on one phone model, we will not chase the rest.” If your three cost you nothing to give up, you have written excuses rather than non-goals. Saying no to good ideas is how the one idea you chose gets finished.

04 · Phase 02
02

Analysis

Analysis turns the high-level idea into something you could actually build from. IBM describes it as moving “from a high-level idea to a practical implementation plan”, which means gathering user requirements, market research, feasibility testing, evaluating prototypes, allocating resources. Microsoft's outputs are system specifications, use-case diagrams, data-flow diagrams.

The word doing all the work is gathering. Requirements are collected from people who have the problem. They are not invented by the team around a table. This is the phase students skip hardest, because inventing is faster and feels more creative. It is also the phase where a single afternoon of effort separates a project that lands from one that quietly misses.

Deliverable A written list of what the software must do, in the words of people who are not on your pod.
In your pod, this week

Find three people who have the problem your project solves. Ask what they do about it now. Write down their exact words. Those words go in the Why we built it section of your page.

Why the exact words

“Farmers cannot identify crop disease early” is your sentence. “I ask my neighbour and by then half the field is gone” is theirs. The second one tells you what to build. The first one does not.

How it fails

IBM warns that teams who skip this “might go through many wasted work cycles before discovering the software's actual needs”. You build for four months, then meet a user in week fifteen. Three messages this week prevent that entirely.

The cheapest phase you will ever run

Three conversations cost you an afternoon. Finding out in November that your project solves a problem nobody has costs you the semester. This is the best return on time anywhere in the life cycle, and it needs no code, no laptop and nobody's permission. You can start it today.

05 · Phase 03
03

Design

Design decides the shape of the thing before you build it. Navigation, screens, how data is stored, which parts talk to which other parts. IBM's list is “outlining the software's navigation, user interfaces, database design and more”, collected into a software design document that developers use as a roadmap while coding.

Two ideas from this phase are worth more to you than the document. The first is modular design, meaning self-contained units of code that each do one job. IBM's reason for it is not elegance, it is throughput. Modules “enable developers to reuse existing modules and work on multiple parts of the software at once, which can reduce bottlenecks”. On a pod of five, that is the difference between five people working and one person working while four wait.

The second is threat modelling, and it belongs here rather than at the end. More on that in section 14.

Deliverable Sketches of every screen, and a decision about which file each piece of code goes in.
In your pod, this week

On paper, draw every screen of your demo. Then list the files you expect in src/ and put one person's name next to each. That list is your design document. Paper counts.

Design is a division of labour

Two people cannot build the same project at once until somebody decides where the seams are. That decision is design, and it is why five people with no design produce less than two people with one.

How it fails

Everyone opens demo/index.html at the same time. Two people write the same function, both edit the same lines, and GitHub asks you to resolve a conflict you never needed to have.

Stop and think · 03

Your pod has five people and one HTML file. What is the actual limit on how fast you can go, and which phase sets it?

Two minutes. Name the phase.

What I was listening for

Design sets it. The limit is not typing speed, it is the number of pieces that can be worked on independently.

One file means one editor at a time and a merge conflict every time you break that rule. Five files with five owners means five people working at once, and the integration cost gets paid once, deliberately, instead of continuously by accident.

This is why the design phase survives in teams that have thrown out every other document. It is not paperwork. It is the thing that makes your whole pod useful at the same time.

06 · Phase 04
04

Coding

The phase everyone thinks is the whole job. Code gets written from the design document and the requirements, interfaces get built, and the project starts producing something you can open. Microsoft names the output plainly. Functional software, a code repository, version control records.

Note that last one. The commit history is a deliverable of this phase. Not a side effect. If your project exists only as files on one laptop, the coding phase has produced code but no record of it, and every question starting with “when did this break” becomes unanswerable. Commit often and your past self keeps helping your future self.

Deliverable Working code in the repository, with a commit history that shows how it got there.
In your pod, this week

Build in demo/index.html and src/. Commit on every day you write code, even when it is broken. A broken commit is recoverable. An uncommitted week is not.

Reviews start here

IBM notes that many teams run code reviews during this phase rather than waiting for testing, because a bug caught by a teammate on Tuesday is far cheaper than the same bug caught by a user in November. Read each other's work. It is the fastest way to improve.

How it fails

Coding starts on day one and never stops. No requirements, no design, no tests, and every question answered by writing more code. That is the big bang model, and section 11 explains why it only works for tiny things.

07 · Phase 05
05

Testing

Testing is the phase where you actively try to make your own software fail. Unit, integration, system and acceptance testing, plus a look for security holes. Then you fix what was found and send it back to be tested again, a loop inside a loop. Finding your own bug is a win, not an embarrassment.

Two numbers from IBM are worth holding on to. Software testing can account for almost 33% of system development costs. Nobody simply cuts it, because skipping testing does not remove the cost, it moves the cost to the phase where it is most expensive to pay. The opposite failure is real too. Teams chase a perfect release and never ship.

The modern answer to that tension is continuous testing. IBM puts it this way. “Testing is not limited to a single phase of the SDLC. Rather, code is tested throughout the entire software development process.” Testing sits at phase five because diagrams need an order. In practice it runs alongside everything.

Deliverable A written record of what you tried, what broke, and what you did about it.
In your pod, this week

Your test/ folder is empty. Put one file in it listing every step a visitor takes through your demo, and what should happen at each step. Walk it yourself. Note every place reality differs.

The test that finds the most bugs

Hand your phone to someone who has never seen the project and say nothing. Do not help. Watch where they get stuck. Ten minutes of this beats an hour of clicking your own happy path.

How it fails

Only the author tests it, only on their own machine, only doing the thing it was built to do. Every real user starts by doing something you never tried, which is exactly why their five minutes are worth so much.

Stop and think · 04

Testing is a third of the cost. Your project has no budget and no QA team. So what is the honest version of testing for a pod, and what are you giving up?

Three minutes. Be specific about the trade.

What I was listening for

The honest version is one written walkthrough and one stranger, repeated every lap. Forty minutes a fortnight, and it catches most of what would embarrass you at a demo. That is a very good trade.

What you give up is real coverage. You will not catch the rare path, the slow network, the odd browser, or anything that only appears under load. You are trading breadth for the two or three failures that hit the most people, and for a student project that is the right call.

The part that is not optional is writing it down. An untested project and a project tested only in someone's memory are the same project on the day that person is unavailable.

08 · Phase 06
06

Deployment

Deployment puts the software where users can reach it. IBM makes the point that this is not only a technical act. “The goal of this phase isn't just to get the software into the hands of users.” It is that users understand how to use it, with minimal disruption. Manuals, training, a beta release to a limited group before general availability.

You have an advantage most professional teams do not. Your deployment is a commit. Press the green button, wait two minutes, and the change is live at your pod address for anyone in the world. No server, no pipeline, no release meeting. The most expensive step in most projects is nearly free in yours. Use it, and let your project be visible from week three.

Deliverable A live address a stranger can open, that shows the current state of the work. 
# your permanent address, fixed for the whole project
https://gcst-ai.github.io/gcst/projects/pod-NN/          the project page
https://gcst-ai.github.io/gcst/projects/pod-NN/demo/     the thing actually running
In your pod, this week

Check that both addresses load and neither still says “Pod NN”. If the placeholder text is there, you have published a template rather than deployed a project. Changing it takes two minutes.

Run a beta

Send the demo link to five people outside your pod before you show it in class. That is exactly the limited release IBM describes, and it costs you one message.

How it fails

Everything works locally and nobody opens the real URL. Then demo day becomes your first deployment, in front of an audience, which is the worst possible moment to find a broken image path.

09 · Phase 07
07

Maintenance

The cycle does not end at deployment. Maintenance is the work after it. Updates, optimisations, unanticipated changes, patches, new use cases, and the bugs your users find that you never would have. IBM's image is a house. Over time small pieces get misused or break down, and they get replaced and hopefully improved.

In DevOps teams, maintenance stops being a phase at all. With continuous integration and continuous delivery, code is added, tested and deployed continuously, and maintenance becomes “an ongoing activity rather than a distinct phase”. Your setup already works this way whether you noticed or not. Every commit is a deployment, so you have been maintaining since your first one.

Deliverable The project still works next month, and the known problems are written down somewhere findable.
In your pod, this week

Start a Known problems list, in your notes or as GitHub issues. Every bug you find and do not fix today goes on it. A bug nobody wrote down is a bug you rediscover in November.

The arrow back to planning

What users do with v0.1 becomes the input to your next plan. That loop is what makes this a cycle rather than a queue. If nothing you learned changed the plan, either nobody used it or nobody asked them.

How it fails

The project is declared finished on demo day and quietly rots. Six months later a link on your CV opens a broken page. Keep it alive and that same link becomes the strongest thing on there.

10 · The practical bit

Your pod folder is already the life cycle

You were given a folder with six things in it, and that list is not arbitrary. Each one is the resting place of a phase. Nobody told you in week one because it would have meant nothing then. It should mean something now.

projects/pod-NN/
  index.html      planning + analysis, written for a reader
  style.css       design, the visual half
  images/         design, the sketches and the cover
  src/            coding
  test/           testing
  demo/           coding + deployment, the thing actually running

This is the practical claim of the whole page. You do not adopt the SDLC. You are already running it, in an order nobody chose. All you have to add is noticing which phase you are in, and leaving the deliverable somewhere the rest of your pod can find it. That is a small change in habit with a large effect on what you finish.

A rule that will save you an argument

If a piece of work is not in the repository, it does not exist. Not a screenshot in the group chat, not a decision somebody remembers, not a design in one person's notebook. The repository is the only shared memory your pod has, and the only one that survives a lost phone.

11 · Models

The models: same phases, different order

Every model on this list runs all seven phases. They differ on the order, how many times each runs, and how much has to be finished before the next one starts. IBM lists eight in common use. You do not need to memorise them, you need to recognise them.

ModelHow it runs the phasesUse it when
WaterfallEach phase finished before the next starts, once.Requirements are stable and stakeholders only want to appear at major milestones.
V-modelWaterfall, with a matching test phase for every build phase.Stable requirements plus a real need for heavy verification.
AgileShort sprints, each producing a small released change.Clients can and will join frequent reviews. Requirements will move.
LeanAgile, tuned to remove waste and delay decisions until the data is in.You have too much process and not enough output.
IterativeBuild a minimum viable product fast, then improve it in successive versions.You know roughly what to build but not exactly how it should behave.
SpiralObjectives, risk analysis, build and test, plan the next loop. Repeat.High risk or high complexity, where frequent correction is expected.
Big bangStart coding. No planning, no requirements analysis.Tiny, self-explanatory projects. High risk everywhere else.
RADFast prototypes shown to users instead of a long planning period.You have users available regularly and need to adapt to them quickly.

Microsoft narrows the same field to four worth comparing and adds one the list above leaves out, DevOps, where development and operations merge and every phase becomes continuous. IBM's framing is precise. DevOps “addresses the rigidity of the SDLC by making each phase of the software development process continuous throughout the project”, so planning, coding, testing, deploying, maintaining and monitoring all run at once rather than in turn.

Retirement is worth ten seconds of your attention. Software ends. Somebody decides to switch it off, move the users, and archive the data. Your project will reach that point too, and “the pod graduated” is not a plan for it.

IBM's guidance on choosing comes down to three questions, and you can answer all three today. Are the requirements clearly defined or likely to change? How complex is the project? How experienced is the team?

12 · Your project

What your pod should actually run

Answer IBM's three questions honestly. Requirements, not defined, and certain to change once you talk to a real user. Complexity, moderate. Team experience, this is your first project. None of that is a weakness. It just narrows the choice, which is what a good question does.

That combination rules out waterfall and the V-model, because both bet on requirements you do not have. It rules out big bang, because five people coding with no plan produce five incompatible halves. Spiral is overkill for a project where nothing catches fire when it breaks. What is left is iterative, with the discipline of agile. A short lap, ending in a deployment, every time.

Fixed

The lap length. Two weeks, every time. If the work does not fit, cut the work. Never extend the lap.

Fixed

The lap ends in a deployment. Something at your pod URL is different from two weeks ago, and a stranger can see it.

Variable

Everything else. Which features, who does what, how polished. This is the part you are allowed to get wrong, because the next lap is only two weeks away.

13 · AI

Where AI sits in the cycle

The IEEE reports that 35% of organisations use AI to aid or accelerate software development, and it is not confined to coding. IBM's examples run across the whole cycle. Identifying requirements in planning, finding trends in user feedback and flagging compliance issues in analysis, drafting first-pass code from a functional design, generating test cases and analysing patterns in test failures.

“Vibe coding” gets named directly and described accurately. Developers describe what they want in plain text, the tool produces the code, and “developers often take this code as a starting point and refine it further”. Starting point. Not output. The difference between those two words is most of your grade.

Then IBM names the cost. This is the part worth memorising, because it is your semester it is describing.

“AI tools might make mistakes and write unoptimized code. If developers do not carefully review all code generated by AI tools, these tools can introduce costly software bugs that are not discovered until much later in the lifecycle.” IBM · AI in the SDLC

Look at where that lands on the cycle. A bug introduced in phase four and found in phase seven has passed through testing and deployment and had more code built on top of it. This is exactly what the life cycle exists to prevent, and an unreviewed AI suggestion walks you into it at speed.

The second cost is subtler. Writing gets faster, so review and validation become the bottleneck, and IBM notes this “can potentially reverse those time savings”. You do not get to keep the speed and skip the reading. Used well, these tools are a genuine advantage. Used as a substitute for understanding, they quietly take the learning away.

The rule

Do not commit code you cannot explain. If a teammate points at a line and asks what it does, and the honest answer is “the model wrote it”, it does not go in. Ask the tool to explain it, then it can.

Where it genuinely helps you

The phases you are weakest at. Turning three messy user conversations into a requirements list. Generating a test walkthrough. Explaining an error message you have never seen. All analysis and testing rather than coding.

Where it will hurt you

Four hundred lines of demo code in an afternoon that nobody on the pod understands. It looks like progress in week three and becomes a wall you cannot climb in week nine.

14 · Security

Security is not a phase

The old arrangement treated security as a stage near the end, somewhere inside testing. DevSecOps, or “secure DevOps”, moves it into every phase instead. IBM says it “incorporates security considerations into every phase of the SDLC”, threading code reviews and penetration testing throughout, so risk is handled early rather than discovered late enough to delay a release.

Microsoft describes the same spread phase by phase. Security goals set in planning, threats assessed in analysis, protocols embedded in design, secure coding standards in development, security tests in testing, secure configuration at deployment, continuous monitoring in maintenance.

This one applies to you today

Your repository is public. Everything you commit is readable by anyone, forever, including after you delete it, because the history keeps it. Never commit an API key, a password, a token, or personal data about someone you interviewed. If you have already committed one, tell a maintainer today and treat that key as compromised. Nobody will be annoyed with you for reporting it. This is the most likely security incident in this class, and one decision made now prevents all of it. No secret goes in the repo.

One more from IBM, because it explains why maintenance is a security phase and not only a bug-fixing one. Supply chain attacks are rising, and a common route is hijacking the update process to ship malware instead of an update. In 2020 the vendor SolarWinds was breached and malicious code went out to its customers disguised as a legitimate update, reaching US government departments. Your update mechanism is a delivery pipeline into every user you have, which is exactly what makes it worth attacking.

15 · Failure modes

Four ways this goes wrong

IBM names four challenges that endanger SDLC projects. All four have a student-sized version, and you will meet at least two before November. Knowing their names in advance is most of the defence.

01 · Scope creep

Requirements expand past the plan, effort goes with them, and the additions “do not serve the core purpose of the software”. Your version is every idea from the group chat becoming a feature while the original one never gets finished.

02 · Poorly defined requirements

Without real analysis, teams “go through many wasted work cycles before discovering the software's actual needs”. Your version is four weeks of code, then one conversation with a user that undoes all of it.

03 · The wrong amount of testing

Too little and users find the bugs. Too much and the release never happens. Your version is almost always too little, right up to the week before the demo, when it suddenly becomes too much.

04 · Insecure maintenance

The update path is an attack path. Your version is smaller and more likely. A leaked key in the commit history, or a dependency you added once and never looked at again.

IBM's sharpest line on all of this is about perfectionism. “For most software development lifecycles, the perfect is the enemy of the good. A functional, if not perfect, release can get to market more quickly, and it can be improved through iterative rounds of postdeployment maintenance.”

That is not permission to ship rubbish. It is the observation that a working thing in front of users generates information, and a perfect thing on your laptop generates none. If you are waiting until your project is good enough to show, you have the order backwards. Showing it is how it gets good.

16 · Do this now · 40 minutes

Run one lap of all seven phases

Not a small version of the cycle. All seven, tonight, at low quality. Low quality is the point. Once you have been round the loop you will know what each phase feels like, and the second lap is always easier than the first.

  1. Planning. Write the one sentence for <p class="summary"> in your index.html. Then write three non-goals in your notes.
  2. Analysis. Message one person outside your pod who has the problem. Ask what they do about it now. Put their words in the Why we built it section.
  3. Design. Sketch the demo on paper. List the files you expect in src/ and put a name against each.
  4. Coding. Open demo/index.html and make one real thing work. A form, a button, one calculation. Small and finished beats large and half done, every time.
  5. Testing. Create a file in test/ listing the steps a visitor takes and what should happen. Walk it. Write down what did not match.
  6. Deployment. Commit. Wait two minutes. Open https://gcst-ai.github.io/gcst/projects/pod-NN/demo/ on your phone, not your laptop.
  7. Maintenance. Start the Known problems list with everything from step 5 you did not fix. Then decide which one the next lap starts with.

Before you close the laptop

These tick boxes save in your browser. They are for you, nobody sees them.

If you only do one

Do number two. Analysis has the highest return and the lowest cost, and it is the one every pod in this class will skip. One conversation this week is worth more than everything else on the list combined, and it is the easiest thing here to actually do.
